# Vendors shipped the diagrams; toggles remain open

Your exact concept does not exist, but its core format does, and most of that format arrived in the last ten weeks. **Auth0's IdentiFlows** (launch post 29 September 2026) already offers step-through sequence diagrams for 13 flows, and **Ping's AuthPlayground** (24 August 2026) pairs static sequence diagrams with executable steps for CIMD, PAR, DPoP and other newer specs. This research found no general OAuth tool where one diagram changes as you flip PKCE, PAR or DPoP; MCPJam does that only for MCP registration and needs a live MCP server. No hosted tool shows one flow at both a simple and an advanced level, no site spans authn and authz across levels of depth, and no official or community tool visualises protocol flows for Keycloak. My recommendation is to build, but smaller than you described: one registration flow (pre-registered, DCR, CIMD) with Keycloak annotations, justified first by your own teaching need. A broad public "visual OAuth" site would compete where Auth0 is moving fastest, for demand that is unproven: recent tool launches scored 3 to 19 Hacker News points while article explainers reach hundreds. Every "not found" here comes from searching rather than proof of absence, and Reddit could not be read.

## Auth0 and Ping took the format, but nobody has reactive toggles

Roughly 40 interactive OAuth and OIDC tools exist, and they split into two camps that rarely overlap: debuggers have toggles but no diagram, and explainers have diagrams but no toggles. The table measures the closest tools against your concept. "Not listed" means this research did not find it. Most entries were operated in a browser; MCPJam was read from docs and source without running its UI, and Authlete was opened only to its first screen.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Your concept** | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes | Both | Yes |
| [IdentiFlows](https://identiflows.dev/) (Auth0) | Yes | Yes, Prev/Next | No | No | No | Not listed | Yes |
| [AuthPlayground](https://www.authplayground.dev/) (Ping) | Static Mermaid | Execute per step; diagram does not track | No | No | No | CIMD only | Yes |

**IdentiFlows is the real competitive risk.** Its Interactive Mode advances a true sequence diagram one message at a time, and clickable arrows show the exact request, response or token ([How it works](https://identiflows.dev/how-it-works)). Variants are separate pages, so Code and Code + PKCE are different diagrams, and the page has no selects or switches in its DOM ([PKCE page](https://identiflows.dev/diagrams/oauth-authorization-code-pkce/)). It ships quickly: the launch post listed 8 flows and asked for feedback, and client credentials, device and private_key_jwt were live a month later ([launch post](https://auth0.com/blog/introducing-identiflows-a-visual-reference-for-identity-flows); [sitemap](https://identiflows.dev/sitemap.xml)). None of its 13 flows covers CIMD, DCR or PAR. Adding toggles looks like a natural next step for it, though that is my judgement, as no public roadmap surfaced.

**AuthPlayground covers your topics with a different interaction.** Its 11 flows include CIMD, PAR, DPoP, Token Exchange and RFC 9728, each with raw request and response panels ([home](https://www.authplayground.dev/)). Its Mermaid diagram did not change when steps were executed ([CIMD flow](https://www.authplayground.dev/flows/cimd/)), the DPoP page offers no user options ([DPoP flow](https://www.authplayground.dev/flows/dpop/)), and the backend is a simulation ([About](https://www.authplayground.dev/about/)).

The remaining tools each prove one part of your idea. **MCPJam builds its diagram from the chosen protocol version and registration strategy**, so the core technique, a function from options to diagram steps, is already demonstrated ([source](https://github.com/MCPJam/inspector/blob/main/mcpjam-inspector/client/src/components/oauth/OAuthSequenceDiagram.tsx)). Authlete has the widest option surface, from PAR and RAR to CIMD, DCR and OpenID Federation, with a live preview of the wire request ([GitHub](https://github.com/authlete/oauth-playground)). ProtocolSoup shows how much a toggle can teach: on Client Credentials, Bearer produced 12 flow events and 2 wire exchanges, while DPoP produced 27 and 4, including the nonce retry ([Looking Glass](https://protocolsoup.com/looking-glass)). The nearest thing to a depth mode is Token Journey's Narrative and Technical views, in a repository with 0 stars, 2 commits and no hosted version ([GitHub](https://github.com/joevanhorn/token-journey)). A wave of near-identical zero-star simulators in 2026 suggests a basic animated flow is now cheap to build, so a plain animator will not stand out.

## The explainer canon is large, beginner-heavy and fixed at one depth

Attention has held for years. An OAuth explainer or discussion passed 139 Hacker News points in every year from 2019 ([431](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=21352045)) through 2026 ([231](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47096520)), and the seven largest OAuth-specific videos total **about 6.8M views**, led by [Barbettini's talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=996OiexHze0) at 2.05M. The field sorts into eight kinds.

The static canon shares a shape. Okta's author calls his post a [high-level overview](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=21355965), a reader found DuckTyped [too conceptual to write an integration from](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45016810), and Hexclave [says it is not for production implementers](https://www.hexclave.com/blog/oauth-from-first-principles). **OAuth 2.1 goes unmentioned** in [Parecki's article](https://aaronparecki.com/oauth-2-simplified/), [oauth.com](https://www.oauth.com/), [Okta's guide](https://developer.okta.com/blog/2019/10/21/illustrated-guide-to-oauth-and-oidc), [ByteByteGo's guide](https://bytebytego.com/guides/oauth-2-explained-with-siple-terms/) and [Hexclave](https://www.hexclave.com/blog/oauth-from-first-principles). Attacking outdated implicit-flow advice would be weak positioning, because the prominent sources now label it: [Barbettini's description](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=996OiexHze0) warns about it, and [DigitalOcean's 2014 tutorial](https://www.digitalocean.com/community/tutorials/an-introduction-to-oauth-2) gained a deprecated-grants warning in January 2026.

Reported confusion is mostly conceptual and mostly about why. OAuth versus OIDC, token types and the reason for the extra steps recur across forums: [JWT vs OAuth](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/39909419/what-are-the-main-differences-between-jwt-and-oauth-authentication) has about 571k Stack Overflow views, [access vs refresh tokens](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/3487991/why-does-oauth-v2-have-both-access-and-refresh-tokens) about 315k. Someone who taught OAuth to hundreds of developers concluded: "OAuth confuses people, and for completely understandable reasons" ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=35721079)). Keycloak adds a mapping layer that generic explainers ignore, with heavy traffic on [invalid redirect_uri](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/45352880/keycloak-invalid-parameter-redirect-uri) (505k), [endpoints](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/28658735/what-are-keycloaks-oauth2-openid-connect-endpoints) (375k), [client secrets](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/44752273/do-keycloak-clients-have-a-client-secret) (230k), [resources, scopes, permissions and policies](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/42186537/resources-scopes-permissions-and-policies-in-keycloak) (181k) and [logout](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/46689034/logout-user-via-keycloak-rest-api-doesnt-work) (163k). These views accumulate over up to 16 years and no survey ranks the topics, so they show persistence rather than current share.

## Keycloak and registration are the strongest of the remaining openings

Coverage is uneven in ways that should decide your scope. "Left open" reflects what this research found, and several thin rows rest on limited searches.

| Area | State today | Left open |
|---|---|---|
| Code + PKCE, JWT decoding, WebAuthn demos, ReBAC sandboxes | Saturated: [IdentiFlows](https://identiflows.dev/), [jwt.io](https://www.jwt.io/), [webauthn.me](https://webauthn.me), [SpiceDB](https://play.authzed.com/) | Nothing worth a new build |
| PAR, RAR, Token Exchange, ID-JAG, DPoP, device, CIBA | One or two interactives each: [AuthPlayground](https://www.authplayground.dev/), [xaa.dev](https://xaa.dev), [dpop.info](https://dpop.info), [IdentiFlows](https://identiflows.dev/) | Combining them in one flow |
| OAuth vs OIDC, ID token vs access token | Top reported confusion. Text at [oauth.net](https://oauth.net/id-tokens-vs-access-tokens/); tools let you inspect tokens without teaching the distinction | A view of which token goes where and why |
| CIMD vs DCR | Vendor prose ([Auth0](https://auth0.com/blog/cimd-vs-dcr-mcp-registration/), [WorkOS](https://workos.com/blog/mcp-client-registration-cimd-vs-dcr), [Scalekit](https://www.scalekit.com/blog/how-mcp-clients-register-using-cimd)) and a static [MCP spec sequence](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/draft/basic/authorization) with branches | Setup-free comparison on one diagram |
| Logout and session management | Static [Curity article](https://curity.io/resources/learn/openid-connect-logout/); OAuth Tools has a logout runner, variant unverified | Interactive front- and back-channel comparison |
| mTLS, FAPI 2.0, OAuth 2.1, GNAP | Text or RFC ASCII ([RFC 8705](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8705), [FAPI](https://openid.net/wg/fapi/specifications/), [OAuth 2.1](https://oauth.net/2.1/), [oauth.xyz](https://oauth.xyz/), whose playground is unconfirmed) | Composition: FAPI 2.0 as PAR + PKCE + sender-constraining, OAuth 2.1 as a diff over 2.0 |
| Keycloak concepts | Reference text and screenshots with a few figures ([Admin Guide](https://www.keycloak.org/docs/latest/server_admin/index.html), [Authorization Services](https://www.keycloak.org/docs/latest/authorization_services/index.html)) | Nearly everything, including protocol-to-setting mapping |
| UMA; RBAC vs ABAC vs ReBAC | Text ([OpenFGA](https://openfga.dev/docs/authorization-concepts), [Oso](https://www.osohq.com/academy)) | A UMA sequence; the comparison before any vendor's DSL |
| Authn and authz across levels | [Logto's wiki](https://auth-wiki.logto.io/) is the broadest glossary; Auth0 spreads across separate microsites | One site with a shared depth model |

**Keycloak is where your expertise counts most and competition is thinnest.** A filename and alt-text filter over the 291 images in the [Admin Guide](https://www.keycloak.org/docs/latest/server_admin/index.html) found no conceptual realm, client or scope figure, though this was not a full image audit. The [FlowDiagram component](https://github.com/keycloak/keycloak/blob/main/js/apps/admin-ui/src/authentication/components/FlowDiagram.tsx) is a configuration tool inside the admin console, and the official [FAPI playground](https://github.com/keycloak/keycloak-playground/tree/main/fapi-playground) has toggles but needs local Keycloak, Quarkus and hosts-file setup, with no diagrams. No tool found maps a protocol option to the realm or client setting that controls it, and vendor sites have little reason to build that.

**Registration is the best first flow because the spec just moved.** MCP's 2026-07-28 revision deprecates DCR in favour of CIMD, naming no removal version ([MCP blog](https://blog.modelcontextprotocol.io/posts/2026-07-28/)). CIMD is SHOULD, DCR is MAY, and clients try pre-registration, then CIMD, then DCR, then prompt the user ([client registration](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2026-07-28/basic/authorization/client-registration)). A twelve-month minimum deprecation window ([changelog](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2026-07-28/changelog)) puts the earliest removal around mid-2027 if the clock started at this revision, which is my inference, so the migration story stays relevant for at least a year. The picture underneath is unsettled, which makes it teachable: CIMD remains IETF working group draft -02 with **no last call recorded** ([Datatracker](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-oauth-client-id-metadata-document/history/)) while MCP still links -00. Keycloak's nightly docs mark CIMD, RFC 8707 and MCP 2026-07-28 compliance as experimental, and say Keycloak rejects ChatGPT's CIMD documents over a metadata field name ([Keycloak](https://www.keycloak.org/nightly/securing-apps/mcp-authz-server)). Among the tools found, only MCPJam and Authlete handle both CIMD and DCR, the first needing a real MCP server and the second drawing no diagram.

Two broader openings deserve caution. Authz has sandboxes rather than explainers, but adding it multiplies the content, so it belongs later. Neutrality also differentiates less than it first appears: the headline tools are vendor-run, but [ProtocolSoup](https://github.com/ParleSec/ProtocolSoup) and [oauthflows](https://github.com/aiauthz/auth-illustration) are independent and open source. Neutral alone is not a position; neutral plus Keycloak mapping is.

## Demand is strong for explanation and unproven for a public tool

Difficulty is the best-supported finding. [Why is OAuth still hard in 2023?](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=35713518) drew 642 points and 282 comments, and an [application security practitioner](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=36490346) and a [daily user in 2026](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47097117) report the same struggle. The evidence is qualitative and self-selected. Some commenters [find the RFCs straightforward](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45017703), and several now [use LLMs for use-case-specific answers](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45018132). LLMs are the real competitor to any new explainer, so yours must offer what a chat answer cannot: a stable visual frame and content verified against a real server.

Launch evidence leans against a tool-first release, weakly. [AuthPlay](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=42618161) scored 3 points, [ProtocolSoup](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46615649) 4 and [Toolkit](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47301023) 6; [workflows.guru](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=44154281) reached 19 and may be a static article, and the best found was a [2014 OAuth Playground post](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=8580567) at 66. Single scores mean little, since Okta's guide got [3](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=21313826) and [5](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=21321394) points before its 431. Points also miss quiet reach: Transformer Explainer has [105 points](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=41221718) yet [9,071 stars](https://github.com/poloclub/transformer-explainer) and an author-reported [490,000+ users](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04619). No OAuth tool publishes usage data. An essay with embedded diagrams looks likelier to travel than a Show HN, though that is an untested inference.

The benchmarks set realistic expectations. No canonical page carries the title "visual guide to transformers"; the likely candidates are [Alammar's static post](https://jalammar.github.io/illustrated-transformer/), [Transformer Explainer](https://poloclub.github.io/transformer-explainer/), [Bycroft's LLM Visualization](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=38505211) and [3Blue1Brown's lesson](https://www.3blue1brown.com/lessons/gpt). Protocol explainers top out around **400 to 600 points** ([Illustrated TLS 568](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=18400158), [How HTTPS Works 466](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=24476469), [Raft 411](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=32484584)). Alammar's post drew [500 points seven years after publication](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46357675) with no built-in interactivity, so clarity sets the bar more than interaction does. None of the benchmarks opened uses a sequence diagram as its core medium, which leaves that format unproven at this level. Your instinct that OAuth outgrows a linear page has support: Illustrated TLS's author said the happy path alone [ran to 400 KB of HTML](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=18201894) and left out client certificates to avoid more density.

The internal case is the most direct fit and the least quantified. Practitioners keep building [personal slide decks](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45014923) and [conference talks](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=35721491), and [Codecentric's Florian Wiech](https://www.codecentric.de/wissens-hub/blog/my-keycloak-learning-journey) describes learning Keycloak through an internal workshop and says its docs do not explain IAM. The one detailed success story is a trainer whose materials [offered a menu of recommended options per use case and why each one solved the problem](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=35722151), which closely resembles presets over toggles. People also [forget the details within months](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=42843464), and Illustrated TLS's author [uses his own site as a reference](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=31121681), so a page you return to may matter more than a one-time walkthrough. This rests on anecdotes plus your own need. Reddit could not be read, and two relevant Keycloak forum threads ([one](https://forum.keycloak.org/t/book-course-or-tutorial/21106), [two](https://forum.keycloak.org/t/feedback-re-dealing-with-keycloak-experience/3625)) refused automated fetches, so they are worth reading by hand.

## The cost sits in keeping toggles true, not in the renderer

**No mature library does step-through plus composable toggles**, so expect a small custom renderer. Mermaid's [sequence docs](https://mermaid.js.org/syntax/sequenceDiagram.html) mention no animation, a [stepping request](https://github.com/mermaid-js/mermaid/issues/3029) has been open since 2022, and the [ordered step animation merged on 27 October](https://github.com/mermaid-js/mermaid/pull/8470) covers flowcharts only. D2 has the closest [variant model](https://d2lang.com/tour/composition/), with looping [playback](https://d2lang.com/tour/composition-formats/) only. [SeqShow](https://github.com/KunxianWang/SeqShow) and [Cinegram](https://github.com/panset/cinegram) add stepping and variants to Mermaid, but have 5 and 7 stars and months of history. Sequence layout is simple, and [Token Journey's viewer](https://github.com/joevanhorn/token-journey/tree/HEAD/viewer) is only about 26.5 KB of JS. A sensible stack, in my judgement, is Astro with MDX, one SVG island driven by typed flow data, and GSAP or Motion. Keep generated Mermaid or static SVG as an export, since you will want these in slides, and encode toggle state in the URL so a slide can link to an exact view.

OAuth toggles cut across messages, so each step and field needs its own condition: DPoP touches several steps and PAR changes what crosses the front channel, which `alt` frames cannot express. Stable step IDs then let a toggle animate as a change rather than a redraw. **That "what changed" transition is the feature no precedent has**, and it should be the centrepiece. The maintenance price is combinatorial, as three toggles already give eight variants, so use named presets, encode invalid combinations as constraints and test each valid one. Verifying against real Keycloak follows Illustrated TLS, whose repo holds a working client, server and captures so [every byte reproduces](https://github.com/syncsynchalt/illustrated-tls13). Its later history shows the risk: the [live page](https://tls13.xargs.org/) still describes X25519 and never mentions ML-KEM, while Cloudflare reports [about 70% of browser traffic](https://blog.cloudflare.com/post-quantum-visibility/) using hybrid ML-KEM. Protocol explainers stay correct and drift from practice, so stamp each flow with its spec version and a last-verified date.

The evidence supports levels of depth but not a page-wide switch. **None of the benchmarks opened has a global simple/advanced mode**; they layer through ordering, in-place expansion and companion pages, as with [Illustrated TLS's Open All](https://tls13.xargs.org/) and [Transformer Explainer's progressive disclosure](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.04619v2). A meta-analysis of 60 experiments found novices gain from high assistance (d = 0.505) and experts from low (d = −0.428), with help for novices the stronger effect ([Tetzlaff et al.](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102142)). Learner control across 18 studies showed almost no effect, g = 0.05 ([Karich et al.](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.3102/0034654314526064)). Neither study tested a self-selected toggle on an explanatory page, and no study found did. The practical reading is to default to the simple view and expand per message with an Open All option, keeping scenario toggles (PKCE on or off) separate from depth controls because they do different jobs. Transformer Explainer's own between-subjects test scored 73.3% against 59.5% for a blog and 61.9% for video (n = 90, builder-run, immediate post-test), and video viewers felt they understood as well despite lower scores ([paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.04619v2)). Felt comprehension is thus a poor signal, which argues for predict-the-next-message prompts in your simple mode.

Effort is the main risk, and almost no creator publishes hours. Transformer Explainer took [over a year with nine authors](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.04619v2). Distill's editors gave [more than 50 hours of diagram help to some articles](https://distill.pub/2021/distill-hiatus/) and cited burnout when they paused in 2021, while still recommending self-publication. My own rough, unsourced bracket for a solo part-time builder is weeks to a few months for one polished flow, and closer to a year for a multi-flow site with toggles and depth. Sequels also earn less: Illustrated TLS's stars fall from [3,513](https://github.com/syncsynchalt/illustrated-tls12) to [921](https://github.com/syncsynchalt/illustrated-tls13), [243](https://github.com/syncsynchalt/illustrated-quic) and [26](https://github.com/syncsynchalt/illustrated-dtls) across later protocols, though those are also more niche, and [The Secret Lives of Data](https://thesecretlivesofdata.com/) never moved past Raft. Plan on the first flow being the whole product until shown otherwise.

## Conclusion

The useful question has moved from "does this exist" to "what survives when Auth0 adds a toggle". The mechanic alone does not survive, because MCPJam shows it is implementable and one product decision at Auth0 could close that gap. What lasts is the layer vendors have little reason to build: Keycloak settings mapped to each message, version skew shown openly, and flows verified against a real server. That changes the build order. Start with the registration flow, make the transition between variants the centrepiece, and send colleagues to IdentiFlows for the basics rather than redrawing them.

Treat the first flow as the test, with your next presentation as the deadline. If you reuse it across audience levels and colleagues return to it unprompted, add a second flow and publish an essay with the diagram embedded. If you stop opening it after the talk, you still have a better slide and can stop there at little loss. If IdentiFlows ships toggles first, drop the general mechanic and keep the Keycloak layer, possibly on an open-source base such as Apache-licensed [ProtocolSoup](https://github.com/ParleSec/ProtocolSoup), whose code has not been assessed for that use.
